Design concepts for feldar.com

Ten ideas for the Feldar home page

Feldar is a writing app for novelists. These are ten early designs for its home page, each with its own look and its own way of showing the product. They are working prototypes: click, type and play with them. Sign-up and links to other pages are switched off.

How you can help

Open a few, spend a minute on each, then tell whoever sent you this link:

  1. Which one made you most want to try Feldar?
  2. After a minute on it, could you say what Feldar does?
  3. What confused or annoyed you, anywhere?

They work on a phone, but a laptop shows more. The bar at the bottom of every page steps between them.

Round three: the newest

Three concepts that explain every feature: what it does, how you use it in three steps, and a real capture of the app. Each gets to the product in the first screen.

Concept H

Then and now

Concept D in one screen. A dial in the hero plays through clay, papyrus, parchment, type, typewriter and screen in about eight seconds and lands on Feldar's page, where the suggestion is live. Then a feature explorer, and the typewriter to close.

The message
The tools keep changing. You stay in charge.
How features are explained
All twelve names in an index; choose one to see its capture beside its steps, on one screen.
Typewriter
The closing section: type a line, then ask for the next one.

Concept I

The novel's ledger

Writing was invented to keep accounts: the first tablets tallied grain, beer and livestock. A novel needs its accounts kept too. A crisp modern account book, with the features as entries behind coloured side tabs.

The message
Feldar keeps the books. You write the book.
How features are explained
Four accounts (Plan, Write, Revise, Keep); every entry opens its steps and capture.
Typewriter
Not used, for contrast.

Concept J

The writer's desk

An illustrated desk full of writing tools from every age: index cards, a corkboard with string, a quill, a red pencil, a clay tablet paperweight. Each object is a feature; click around the desk to explore.

The message
Every tool a writer reaches for, on one desk, now working together.
How features are explained
Each object opens its feature's steps and capture under the desk, with a plain index as well.
Typewriter
The centrepiece of the desk, live.

Round two: the history of writing set

Four concepts that put Feldar at the end of a five-thousand-year line of writing tools. Each one borrows a material from that line and uses it to carry the product's message: your words stay yours, and what Feldar writes waits for your yes.

Concept D

5,000 years

A scroll down the whole line: clay, papyrus, parchment, type, typewriter, word processor, Feldar. One sentence is carried through every era and redrawn in its material, then finished in blue.

The message
The tools keep changing. The writer never has. Every new tool had its doubters; here are the rules Feldar's AI keeps to.
Strongest for
Writers wary of AI. It answers the worry with history, then with rules.
Risk
The product arrives late: six eras come before Feldar does.

Concept E

Clay

The whole page is clay you can press. Type a line and it is pressed into a tablet. Features sit on shelves as tablets. One precious colour, lapis blue, is Feldar's ink.

The message
Wet clay can still change; fired clay is yours for good. A suggestion is wet until you Accept it.
Strongest for
Being remembered. Nothing else in the category looks or behaves like it.
Risk
A strong costume. It has to keep reading as a writing app, not a museum.

Concept F

Scriptorium

An illuminated manuscript, loud and funny: gold leaf, big decorated initials, and creatures in the margins. Ultramarine, the blue that once cost as much as gold, is Feldar's ink.

The message
The margin is where a book gets better. Lens and Checks are the margin talking back.
Strongest for
Fantasy and historical writers, and social sharing: it is the most ornamental of the seven.
Risk
Ornament can crowd the product. Body text must stay plain.

Concept G

Typewriter

A typewriter you can type on. The headline is struck onto the page key by key, your own keys work, and a ribbon lever switches to the second colour.

The message
A two-colour ribbon: black for you, blue for Feldar. The carbon copy is the Writing record.
Strongest for
Play. The hero is a toy that explains the product in one gesture.
Risk
Nostalgia may read as retro software to some visitors.

Round one: the first clean-slate set

Three directions with no shared theme: a book, a desk, a studio.

Concept A

Manuscript

The home page typeset as a book: paper, one serif, a table of contents, chapters with plates. The centrepiece is an annotated manuscript page where every feature is a note in the margin, pinned beside the line it acts on.

The bet
Novelists trust taste. A page that reads like a well-made book earns the hearing an AI tool usually does not get.
Strongest for
Writers wary of AI, and search: the most readable text, in clear chapters.
Risk
Quiet. The app is shown in plates rather than made the star.

Concept B

Index cards

A novelist's desk. Bold type, flat colour, and every feature as a card you could pick up: ruled index cards, sticky notes, a stamp. A coloured tile for each feature, and a rail that walks one chapter through four moves.

The bet
Visitors skim. Make the breadth of the app obvious and inviting in one screen, with a way into each feature.
Strongest for
Paid and social traffic, genre writers, anyone deciding in ten seconds.
Risk
Playfulness can read as lightweight to literary writers.

Concept C

Night studio

The home page is the demo. Dark and focused, with lit manuscript pages. Every section is something to do: continue a line, generate scenes, drag Canvas cards, pick a rewrite, fix a check, ask a reader.

The bet
People believe what they try. Let them use each feature before asking for a sign-up.
Strongest for
Showing how the AI behaves, and standing apart from other AI writing tools.
Risk
The most to build and maintain, and the demos must stay true to the app.

Good to know

  • The words in the demos are made up. Lines shown as Feldar's suggestions were written by hand for these pages, using a sample novel called The Winter Ledger.
  • The screenshots are real. Every capture is the actual app, filled with that sample novel.
  • Pieces can be mixed. If you like the top of one and the middle of another, say so.
